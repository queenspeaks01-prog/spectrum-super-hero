Acceptance & Inclusion

Create spaces where autistic people are respected, included, and able to participate without having to hide who they are.

Respect

Acceptance starts with the person

♥

Listen to autistic people, respect communication differences, and include the person in decisions that affect them.

Inclusion

Remove barriers

↔

Look at the environment, expectations, and access needs—not only at what a person does differently.

  • Offer accessible choices
  • Respect different communication styles
  • Provide reasonable accommodations
  • Include autistic people in decisions
Avoid

Acceptance is not masking

Acceptance does not mean expecting someone to hide harmless autistic traits, force eye contact, or behave in one “normal” way.

Try this: Ask, “What would make this space easier for you to use?”
Ally action

Use your influence

Challenge stereotypes, make information accessible, and speak up when policies or environments exclude people.

Communication

Communication can happen in many forms. The goal is connection and understanding—not making everyone communicate in the same way.

Many forms

Communication can look different

💬

Speech, writing, typing, signs, gestures, pictures, AAC, facial expressions, and other methods can all be meaningful communication.

Helpful habits

Make communication easier

  • Use clear, direct language when helpful
  • Allow processing time
  • Offer visual information
  • Ask how the person prefers to communicate
Important

Don't measure understanding by appearance

Eye contact, facial expression, body language, or spoken language are not reliable measures of intelligence, honesty, or understanding.

Try this: Give the person enough time to respond before repeating or changing the question.
Connection

Communication is two-way

Good communication means adapting on both sides. The person should not have to do all the adapting.

Sensory Experiences

Sensory experiences can be stronger, weaker, or simply different. The same environment can feel comfortable to one person and overwhelming to another.

Understand

Common sensory areas

◌

Sound, light, touch, smell, taste, movement, and body sensations can all affect comfort and participation.

Plan ahead

Make environments more accessible

  • Identify quieter spaces
  • Give advance notice about loud sounds or changes
  • Allow appropriate movement or sensory breaks
  • Reduce unnecessary visual or background stimulation
Individual needs

Ask instead of assuming

Some people avoid certain input; others seek input that helps them regulate, focus, or feel comfortable.

Try this: Ask, “What would make this environment more comfortable?”
Early support

Notice signs of stress

A person may become quieter, move away, ask for a break, cover their ears, seek movement, or communicate in another way when an environment becomes difficult.

Families & Caregivers

Families and caregivers can help create supportive environments by learning who the person is, what they enjoy, how they communicate, and what support works.

Start here

Start with the individual

★

Learn communication methods, preferences, strengths, routines, support needs, and goals. Include the autistic person in decisions as much as possible.

Daily support

Build practical routines

  • Use predictable routines when helpful
  • Prepare for changes and transitions
  • Keep communication supports consistent
  • Make time for interests and rest
Teamwork

Build a support team

Depending on the person's needs and choices, support may involve family, school staff, healthcare professionals, community programs, advocates, or other trusted people.

Remember: Caregivers need support too. A sustainable plan considers the whole family while respecting the autistic person's autonomy.
When needed

Find reliable resources

Use the Resource Finder to look for verified organizations and services. Check eligibility, location, cost, and accessibility before relying on a service.

Educators & Schools

Inclusive education means designing learning environments where students can access instruction, communicate, participate, and demonstrate learning in ways that work for them.

Classroom access

Make expectations clear

✓

Use concise instructions, predictable routines, advance notice of major changes, visual examples, and manageable steps.

Participation

Offer multiple ways to respond

  • Allow speaking, typing, writing, visuals, or other appropriate methods
  • Give processing time
  • Consider sensory access
  • Support transitions
Belonging

Create genuine inclusion

Focus on strengths and interests, avoid public shaming, and do not force eye contact or other harmless behaviors.

Try this: Ask the student what helps them learn and participate, using an accessible way to gather their input.
Accommodations

Remove barriers

Accommodations are tools for access. They are not unfair advantages; they help students participate in learning.

Myths & Facts

Use this quick guide to challenge common misconceptions and build a more accurate understanding of autism.

MYTH

“Autism looks the same in everyone.”

FACT

Autistic people can have very different communication styles, strengths, preferences, sensory experiences, and support needs.

MYTH

“If someone speaks, they don't need communication support.”

FACT

A person can use speech and still benefit from visual information, extra processing time, AAC, or other communication supports.

MYTH

“Eye contact shows whether someone is listening.”

FACT

People can listen and understand without making eye contact. Eye contact should not be used as a test of respect, attention, or honesty.

MYTH

“Accommodations give someone an unfair advantage.”

FACT

Accommodations remove barriers so people can access information, environments, and opportunities.

MYTH

“Acceptance means ignoring support needs.”

FACT

Acceptance and support go together. Respecting someone includes taking their actual access and support needs seriously.

MYTH

“There is one best way to communicate.”

FACT

Communication can include speech, writing, signs, gestures, visuals, typing, AAC, and combinations of methods.
